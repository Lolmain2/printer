# Research notes: vocal warm-ups → extreme vocals

These notes are the basis for the app's curriculum. They summarise what the
voice-science literature and the main teaching methods say, and are honest
about where the evidence is thin. **Nothing here replaces a qualified voice
teacher or a laryngologist / speech-language pathologist.**

## 1. Where the sounds come from

The larynx has two pairs of folds:

- **True vocal folds**: make clean pitched sound. They are delicate, and
  pressing them hard together at high pressure ("pressed phonation", yelling)
  is what injures voices.
- **False vocal folds (ventricular folds)**: sit just above the true folds.
  Normally they stay open while you sing. They are thicker and are not built
  for fine pitch work.

Above those is the **supraglottic** area (arytenoid cartilages, aryepiglottic
folds, epiglottis). Most "rough" or "distorted" sounds are made here.

Findings from laryngoscopy and high-speed imaging:

- High-speed phonoscopy (4,000 frames/s) of heavy-metal growls, screams and
  inhaled sounds found that they are **made mostly by supraglottic tissue, not
  the true vocal folds**. That may be why skilled performers don't injure the
  true-fold mucosa.
- Complete Vocal Technique (CVT) researchers (McGlashan, Sadolin et al.) used
  videolaryngostroboscopy to tie each "effect" to a structure:
  - **Distortion**: mostly the false folds vibrating along with the true folds.
  - **Growl**: the arytenoids vibrating against the epiglottis.
  - **Grunt**: supraglottic structures plus large vocal-fold vibration.
  - **Rattle**: vibration at the arytenoid cartilages.
  - **Creak / creaking**: related to vocal fry. Vocal fry itself is the lowest
    true-fold register, with slow, irregular, "popcorn" vibration.
- In these studies, trained singers showed **no sign of excessive or harmful
  vibration at the glottis** while making the effects. The researchers still
  call the safety question open.

**Caveats:** the studies are small (often 2–32 singers), use *trained* singers,
and many come from the CVT research group. "Can be done safely by trained
singers" does not mean "is safe however you do it".

## 2. The key skill: retraction ("open throat")

Every reputable method teaches you to **keep the true folds from being
squeezed** while the rough sound happens above them:

- **Estill Voice Training** calls this *false-fold retraction*. Its classic
  cue is the **"silent laugh"**: the feeling of laughing silently, which pulls
  the false folds apart. The opposite, *constriction*, is the throat-closing
  feeling of grunting while you lift something heavy.
- **Melissa Cross** (*The Zen of Screaming*) builds screaming on the same idea:
  support, an open throat, and letting the distortion "ride" on air instead of
  being squeezed out.
- **CVT** treats distortion as an *effect* added on top of a healthy basic
  mode (Neutral, Curbing, Overdrive, Edge). You need the clean sound first.

So the app makes you learn **retraction and clean fry before adding any
distortion**, and keeps returning to them.

## 3. Warm-ups: semi-occluded vocal tract (SOVT) exercises

These include lip trills, tongue trills, humming, "ng", and phonating through a
straw (in air or into water).

- **Mechanism (Titze, 2006):** partly closing the lips raises pressure above
  the folds. That strengthens interaction between the vocal folds and the
  vocal tract, so you get the same output with *less collision force* on the
  folds.
- **Evidence:** randomised and sham-controlled trials (Kapsner-Smith et al.
  2015; Meerschman et al. 2019) and studies of ageing voices support SOVT in
  voice therapy. Effects are modest and trials are small. Evidence for SOVT as
  a warm-up specifically is mostly carried over from that therapy research.
- **Sirens and glides** through the whole range, gentle 5-note scales, and
  starting at moderate volume are standard warm-up practice in most methods.

## 4. Common teaching progression (practitioner consensus)

The order in coaching material is very consistent. It isn't peer-reviewed,
but the same steps appear across many coaches:

1. **Clean vocal fry**: the quietest, laziest creaky "uhh" at the bottom of
   your range. It should feel effortless.
2. **Fry with air and an open throat**: add the silent-laugh feeling, open
   the mouth a little like a yawn, and *gently* increase airflow. Grit appears
   without extra squeezing.
3. **Inhale fry** (optional): a quiet fry on the in-breath. Coaches use it to
   feel the false-fold "rattle" with little true-fold load. Keep it soft and
   short, because it dries the throat.
4. **Fry screams**: thinner and sharper. Usually taught first because
   they need the least air and volume. Raise pitch for "highs".
5. **False cord screams**: thicker, lower, roar-like, more chesty. Need more
   support and airflow. Often found by starting clean, adding volume in chest
   voice, and letting distortion engage at the top with the throat open.
6. **Lows / gutturals / growls**: deeper supraglottic sounds that use the
   growl and grunt mechanisms. Advanced.

The same guides list these mistakes again and again: **forcing too much air
and tension**, skipping clean fry, too much volume too early, and pushing
through pain.

## 5. Safety rules the app enforces

| Rule | Why |
|---|---|
| Always warm up (SOVT and sirens) before distortion, and cool down after | Lower-impact phonation first; standard practice in every method |
| Distortion blocks are short (bursts of a few seconds, a few minutes total) and get longer slowly | Overuse is the main risk factor coaches name |
| **Pain = stop.** Tickle, scratch, hoarseness or "raw" feeling = stop distortion for the day | Universal advice; pain means the true folds are being loaded |
| Must pass a check-in before the next stage unlocks | Builds the skill in order; stops "skip to the scream" |
| Pain or hoarseness at a check-in locks distortion until a clean check-in | Rest and recovery |
| Hoarseness lasting more than ~2–4 weeks, voice loss, pain when talking, or blood → see an ENT/laryngologist | Clinical hoarseness guidelines recommend laryngoscopy for persistent hoarseness |
| Hydrate; avoid practising when sick, after shouting, or on a tired voice | Vocal hygiene basics |

## 6. What the app does with this

- Every session = **Warm-up (SOVT)** → **Technique block for your stage** →
  **Cool-down**.
- 7 technique stages follow the progression above. Each one unlocks after
  enough sessions at the previous stage with healthy check-ins.
- Pitch-guided exercises play reference tones (scales, glides, drones) in a
  range you pick (low / mid / high voice).
- After each session you rate how your voice feels. The app uses that to gate
  progress and to tell you to rest.
